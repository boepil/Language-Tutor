## Summary of the story

- bell hooks describes teaching as "the practice of freedom": learning tied to students' real lives, not only to information.
- You read it as a book about not betraying students. Betrayal, for you, is handing them knowledge cut off from their lives and from what they are trying to achieve.
- You also name the matching betrayal on the teacher's side: professors who were valued for their minds and logic while their conduct and moral behavior were set aside.
- She remembers professors who were not interested in enlightenment and who treated the mind as all that counted, even when their conduct was abusive.
- She argues that engaged pedagogy asks teachers to grow too: to be vulnerable, to take the first risk, and to be wholly present in mind, body and spirit.
- Resistance, for her, has to be deliberate and shared, including recovering suppressed knowledge and changing how teaching is done.
- In your words, the teacher has to keep searching for enlightenment and practice his own being, mind and wholeness, not only teach something outside the classroom. The way to do that is by being subversive and by being present.

## Closing synthesis

- Where you started: the Introduction and the early pages of Engaged Pedagogy, where you worked out that the performative act is a change in the student's mind, and that the classroom's excitement comes from collective effort (notebook points 1 and 2).
