## Summary of the story

*Draft for the closing conversation. Kept short, and limited to what the text and our dialogue actually covered. Correct anything that doesn't match how you read it.*

An unnamed narrator, a loner whose father has died, is taken in by a woman, Anna, whom he met on a bench. He empties her room of furniture, piece by piece, into the corridor, and settles on a sofa in what is left. She feeds him and tends the room, and he lives by shutting out everything that could tie him down. Her work (she tells him they "live by prostitution") fills the house with groans and giggles he cannot stand. She then announces she is pregnant; he answers "perhaps it's mere wind", tells her to abort, and denies the child is his. He stays through the autumn, dreading winter, until the birth, which "finished" him. He climbs over the sofa, pushes out through the junk, and leaves while the baby cries behind him. In the street he can still hear the cries, fainter each time. He ends by saying he could have done with other loves, but "there it is, either you love or you don't."

## 1. Closing synthesis

*Built from your own readings, not a fresh retelling.*

**The shape you found.** Across the story you kept noticing one move: the narrator pulls back from anything that would tie him down, and the pulling back keeps failing. You saw it in how he treats the pregnancy, how he treats his father's memory, and how he leaves the house at the birth. By the end you had a reading of your own that fit all three.

**Where you started, and where you ended.**

- *The pregnancy.* You first read his "mere wind" as indifference to the *bother* of a child in the house, not to the child. I pushed: why argue the pregnancy out of existing, then? You allowed that this is something stronger: "he would prefer it would be just a thought." Then you corrected me: not "thought", *fart*. That tipped it toward dark comedy, a man insisting her body is only producing gas.
- *The mountain.* You said at first, "I don't know the relationship to her body so much." Once prompted, you saw it was her rotundities redrawn as terrain.
- *"If I chose" (twice).* Your reading: "Meaning he's not willing to go back, probably causing pain." We agreed this is the same arm's-length move he makes with her and the pregnancy, now applied to his childhood and his father.
- *The graves.* You caught my misreading: "it doesn't give it for the living, it gives it for the dead." You then offered two readings of why graves surface here: winter makes him think of death ("Maybe the winter makes him feel or think about death"), or leaving the house ("Maybe leaving the house?"). The text does not decide between them.
- *The birth.* You read it as the one place his distance breaks. The cries get to him enough that he leaves instead of tolerating them. He scrabbles and barges through the junk "regardless of the clatter", which is physical and frantic where everything before was cool and detached.
- *The ending.* You first read "I could have done with other loves" as meaning he returned to her, then as meaning he *lacked* other loves. I corrected both: nothing in the text has him going back, and the phrase means he could have managed fine with other loves. After that you reached your own reading: *he could have, but he didn't, so it means he still has this love.* You also said it is "love that we don't know if it's finished or still continuing." That fits the cries, which fade ("a little fainter each time") but never stop.

**Your closing line on the story, in your words:** in his unconcerned way he is saying he *could* have done with other loves, but he didn't, and so the love still holds him.

**Threads worth carrying into the next piece.** The father (lighthouse names, the Wains found only because his father showed him once); denial as comedy; the narrator's refusal to look closer ("the colour of an eye half seen, or the source of some distant sound").

## 2. Reading record (dialogue form)

### Earlier sessions (folios 9–32), from handoff notes
- **The hyacinth** (f.32). You offered, unprompted, that the flower's slow withering despite daily watching mirrors the narrator withering inside. It fit the stripped-bare room.
- **"Closer to Giudecca in the hell of unknowing"** (f.32–33). You worked out that the small unanswerable questions (a half-seen eye, a distant sound) are ranked *below* the big ones (God, protoplasm, the self), which Beckett already treats as beneath serious thought. You framed it as: not worth seeking redemption in trivial mysteries.
- **Parsnips and violets** (f.33). You proposed it echoes his relationship with the woman, one underground and functional (parsnip), one visible and ornamental (violet). You tried the mapping both ways (him as parsnip, her as violet; then reversed, since he is the one who asks for the parsnip). Left open; I pressed for textual evidence each time.
- **"A dish of sucking Jew"** (f.33). You asked directly. We confirmed against two outside sources that this is the printed text, not an extraction error. Read as a casually dropped, crude antisemitic trope with no scholarly gloss for the phrase; treated as deliberately shocking rather than symbolically deep.

### Final sessions (folios 33–36), from the transcript
- **Her announcement** (f.33). Pronoun slip ("he" for "she") caught; "impudence" asked. See §1 for the "mere wind" exchange.
- **"One big eye… riveted on the remains of the hyacinth"** (f.34). You asked what "riveted" means and wanted the rivet itself explained (small metal pin, like the studs on jeans pockets). You then tied it back: the hyacinth is "the one that's crumbling, the one that's dying."
- **"Abort, abort, and they'll blush like new"** (f.34). Your reading: abort the pregnancy. Confirmed; no softening on his part.
- **Mountain and heather** (f.34). Covered in §1. You asked what the clinking sounds were (stone-cutters' hammers heard far off).
- **"I dreaded the winter… the snow gives warmth and deadens the tumult"** (f.34–35). Read fluently; "tumult" asked.
- **Walking out, and the junk in the corridor** (f.35). Covered in §1.
- **"I used the word marriage, it was a kind of union in spite of all"** (f.35). You asked whether she was crying because he proposed. No: the cries are the baby's, the birth. "Marriage" is his wry label for the arrangement, not a proposal. You then asked how people actually propose in English: "will you marry me" / "he proposed to her" (not "suggested her to get married").
- **The cries and the Wains** (f.35). You read "on, back, on, back" as him playing with the cries. You noticed the father pattern again: the Wains are the only constellation he can find alone.
- **The ending** (f.36). Covered in §1. My corrections stood; your final reading was your own.

### Open questions we left unresolved
- Parsnip / violet: who is the hidden one, who the ornamental one?
- Why graves surface at "how many graves in her giving, for the living": winter's dread, or leaving the house?
- Is the story's "marriage" fully ironic, or does "union in spite of all" carry something real?
